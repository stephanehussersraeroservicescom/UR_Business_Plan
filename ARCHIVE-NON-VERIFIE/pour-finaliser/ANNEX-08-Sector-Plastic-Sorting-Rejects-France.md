## OBJECTIVE

To answer the question: **Which identifiable plastic waste streams could we target as dedicated feedstock for the sorting operation, and do their producers pay gate fees to dispose of them or sell them as commodities?**

This annex complements the mixed-waste analyses in Annexes 5 (OMR), 6 (CDG Airport), and 7 (DAE Industrial) by focusing on streams that are predominantly or entirely plastic. These streams are relevant for two reasons: (a) they represent the highest-yield feedstock for pyrolysis oil production (65–80% oil yield vs 23% for mixed waste), and (b) they clarify whether the Urban Rig's sorting facility can operate as a standalone profit centre by accepting plastic waste at a gate fee.

The Urban Rig's sorting/pyrolysis operation should target **Category B streams exclusively**. The UR's value proposition is accepting waste that *nobody else wants* and extracting value through thermal conversion.

## 5. CATEGORY B — NEGATIVE-VALUE STREAMS (PRIMARY TARGETS)

These are the streams where producers currently **pay** to dispose of their plastic waste, making them ideal feedstock for the Urban Rig at a gate fee.

### 5.1 Understanding the French Plastic Sorting Chain

Before detailing the individual streams, it is essential to understand how plastic packaging flows through the French recycling system, because two physically distinct "reject" (sorting rejects / refus de tri) streams emerge at different points in the sorting line — one with >95% plastic content, the other mixed material at only 35–45% plastic.

**How the two reject streams diverge inside the sorting centre:**

A modern sorting centre (centre de tri) processes incoming yellow-bin material through a sequence of mechanical and optical stages. First, ballistic separators divide the flow into 2D (flat/film) and 3D (rigid/volumetric) fractions, while magnets and eddy-current separators extract metals. Then NIR (near-infrared) optical sorters — typically TOMRA AUTOSORT or Pellenc Mistral units — spectrally scan each item and fire compressed-air jets to eject identified polymers into category-specific bales ([TOMRA — sorting technology](https://www.tomra.com/waste-metal-recycling/products/machines)). These machines achieve >95% purity on their positive-sort output ([Antonopoulos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162419/) — >99% for PS rigid, ~95% for PE film).

This process produces two types of non-recycled output:

- **Positively sorted plastic with no recycling buyer (→ B1a):** Items the NIR sorter correctly identified and ejected as a specific polymer (PS pot, PE film, opaque PET tray, PP tub, multi-layer pouch), but for which no mechanical recycler currently accepts the material. These items sit in polymer-category bales at >95% plastic purity — the machine confirmed what they are. They are sent to incineration not because they couldn't be sorted, but because no recycling route exists for them. (refus de tri positifs — sorting rejects that are positively identified)

- **Mixed unidentified residual (→ B1b):** Items the optical sorter could not identify — too small, too contaminated, composite, or obscured. These fall through to the residual conveyor along with soiled paper, textile fragments, organic contamination, and fines. Plastic content is only 35–45% because non-plastic contaminants dominate this stream. (refus de tri mixte — mixed sorting rejects)

The Urban Rig targets the B1a stream specifically — acquiring the positive-sort output that the sorting centre has already confirmed as plastic at >95% purity via NIR spectroscopy.

**Why this is the absolute #1 target:**

1. **Confirmed >95% plastic content via NIR optical sorting.** Modern sorting centres (centres de tri) use NIR (near-infrared) optical sorters — typically TOMRA AUTOSORT or Pellenc Mistral — that spectrally identify each item's polymer type before ejecting it with compressed air. These machines achieve >95% purity on positively sorted output ([TOMRA](https://www.tomra.com/waste-metal-recycling/products/machines); [Antonopoulos et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162419/)). The B1a stream consists of items the machine correctly identified as plastic (PS, PE film, opaque PET, PP, multi-layer) but for which no mechanical recycler exists. Because the Urban Rig acquires the *positive sort output* — not the mixed residual — the >95% plastic content is an equipment-verified specification, not an assumption. This is fundamentally different from the general mixed sorting rejects (B1b, §5.3), which consist of material the optical sorter *could not identify* or that fell through before reaching the NIR stage.

2. **Massive volume — 135,000 T/year in IdF alone.** The UR at 46,200 T/year would consume only 34% of the IdF supply. There is no risk of feedstock scarcity.

3. **Already aggregated.** The material sits in bales at the ~200 sorting centres across France (and ~20–30 in IdF). No dispersed collection is needed — the logistics infrastructure already exists.

4. **Disposal is a costly problem for sorting centres.** They currently pay €100–150/T to incinerate this material. The UR can offer €80–120/T and still generate substantial margin from pyrolysis oil — because the oil yield from near-100% plastic feed is 65–80% (vs 23% for OMR).

5. **The over-sorting bottleneck creates opportunity.** CITEO has built only 100 kT/year of over-sorting capacity to handle ~770 kT/year of non-recyclable plastic. The remaining ~670 kT/year has no destination other than incineration. The UR positions itself as an alternative value recovery route for this massive surplus.

6. **Even SRF is underserving this market.** Although SRF (Solid Recovered Fuel / CSR — Combustible de Secours de Récupération, French) preparation exists as an intermediate valorisation (value recovery) route, only ~300 kT/year of SRF is produced nationally — meaning 80% of sorting centre rejects are still NOT valorised (converted to value) as SRF and go straight to incineration at €100–150/T ([L'Usine Nouvelle — SRF](https://www.usinenouvelle.com/article/si-on-se-chauffait-aux-csr-ces-briquettes-a-base-de-dechets-non-recyclables.N935999)). The UR competes not just against incineration but also against SRF — and wins on economics, because pyrolysis oil at €500/T is far more valuable than the thermal energy SRF produces (€30–50/T equivalent).

7. **Regulatory tailwind.** The Anti-Waste Law for a Circular Economy (Loi AGEC — Law on Anti-Waste and Circular Economy) target of "100% recycled plastic by 2025" is unachievable with current infrastructure. If pyrolysis qualifies as chemical recycling, sorting centres routing material to the UR could count it as "recycled" rather than "incinerated" — dramatically improving their reported recycling rates and CITEO compliance.

**Important — two distinct costs borne by two different actors:**

This stream involves two separate economic penalties that apply at different points in the value chain, and understanding the distinction is critical to the UR's commercial strategy:

1. **CITEO eco-modulation penalty (€1,000/T)** — This is a regulatory surcharge paid by the **packaging brand owner** (e.g., Tetra Pak, Danone, Nestlé) to the French EPR scheme (CITEO/Adelphe) for placing non-recyclable or hard-to-recycle plastic packaging on the French market. It is not a disposal fee — it is levied per kilogram of packaging put on market, regardless of what happens to the waste downstream. The penalty exists to incentivise brands to switch to recyclable formats. Source: [Adelphe tariff guide 2024](https://www.adelphe.fr/sites/default/files/Documents/2024-04/rate-guide-2024-modif-avril-2024.pdf).

2. **Physical disposal gate fee (€100–150/T)** — This is what the **sorting centre or waste operator** (e.g., Paprec, Veolia) pays to the incinerator to physically destroy the reject material. The sorting centre collects the multi-layer packaging, cannot mechanically recycle it, and must pay to dispose of it. This is the cost the UR's proposed gate fee of €70–100/T undercuts.

**Why this dual cost structure creates a powerful commercial opportunity:**

If pyrolysis qualifies as "chemical recycling" under the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR), the brand owner's CITEO penalty drops to near zero for material routed to the UR — because the packaging is now "recycled" rather than "non-recyclable." This creates the possibility of a three-way commercial arrangement:

- The **brand owner** pays the UR a premium (€50–150/T) to certify that their packaging was chemically recycled, eliminating their €1,000/T CITEO penalty — a massive net saving
- The **sorting centre** pays the UR a standard gate fee (€70–100/T) to accept the physical material — lower than the €100–150/T they currently pay incinerators
- The **UR** receives revenue from both parties (€120–250/T combined), plus the pyrolysis oil revenue from processing

This makes multi-layer packaging potentially the highest-margin feedstock per tonne, even though the physical gate fee alone (€70–100/T) appears modest.

## 12. RECOMMENDATIONS FOR THE BUSINESS PLAN

1. **Target non-recyclable plastic packaging (B1a) as the primary feedstock — not general sorting rejects (refus de tri).** France's sorting centres (centres de tri) produce ~670 kT/year of plastic that has been collected and sorted but has no mechanical recycling outlet, of which ~135 kT/year is in IdF. This material is 90–100% plastic, already aggregated at sorting centres, and generates €415/T total revenue (gate fee + oil) vs €250/T for OMR — a 66% improvement. The UR at 46,200 T/year would consume only 34% of IdF supply.

2. **Do not compete for Category A (commodity) plastic streams.** Clean post-industrial film and production scrap are more valuable sold to mechanical recyclers. The UR's competitive advantage is in accepting waste that is too contaminated, mixed, or problematic for mechanical recycling.

3. **Build the commercial strategy around "the disposal problem."** Every Category B stream exists because someone has a waste they cannot economically recycle and must pay to dispose of. The UR offers a lower-cost disposal route with the added regulatory benefit of qualifying as thermal treatment / chemical recycling.

4. **Monitor the EU classification of pyrolysis as "recycling" closely.** If pyrolysis of waste plastic is officially classified as recycling (rather than "energy recovery") under the EU Waste Framework Directive and PPWR (Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation), the commercial value proposition transforms: producers can claim recycling credits, CITEO/REP contributions drop, and the UR can charge premium gate fees for "recycling" certificates alongside the physical processing fee.

5. **Leverage the UR's halogen filtration as a competitive moat.** The UR's patented catalytic halogen adsorption system captures both chlorine (from PVC) and bromine (from brominated flame retardants), enabling the UR to accept PVC-rich streams (construction/demolition at 50% PVC) and BFR-contaminated WEEE plastics that competing pyrolysis operators must reject. This expands the addressable market to include all seven Category B streams without restriction and allows the UR to command premium gate fees on hazardous-adjacent material (€200–400/T for BFR+ WEEE).

6. **B1a transforms the business model — and reduces the need for pre-sorting.** The standalone sorting business model (see UR-Standalone-Sorting-Business-Model.xlsx) assumed OMR-composition input requiring extensive sorting. With B1a at 90–100% plastic content, the UR's sorting facility becomes largely unnecessary for this stream — the material is already plastic. This reduces OPEX (fewer sorting staff, less equipment wear) while dramatically increasing revenue (€415/T vs €250/T). The sorting facility remains valuable for B1b (general sorting rejects / refus de tri) and other mixed streams, but B1a could be routed directly to shredding and pyrolysis.

7. **Prioritise testing B1a (non-recyclable plastic packaging) on the UR.** A single characterisation + pyrolysis trial run with actual plastic sorting rejects would validate the 90–100% plastic content, confirm the polymer mix (PS/PE/PP/PET distribution), and measure the actual oil yield (expected 60–65%). This single data point would anchor the financial model on measured data and, if confirmed, would transform the UR's business case — shifting projected revenue from €11.55M/year (OMR basis) to ~€19M/year (B1a basis).
