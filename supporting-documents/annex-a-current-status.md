## A.3 Residual Waste: Non-Recyclable and Non-WTE

### Handling of Remaining Non-Recyclable Mixed Waste

#### Conventional Options for Mixed Residual Waste

**Landfilling**:
- Long-term methane emissions
- Groundwater risks
- Permanent space consumption
- No material recovery

**Incineration**:
- Reduces volume but produces toxic ash
- Produces dioxins
- Requires pollution control
- Metals often oxidized and lost

---

#### Urban RIG Advantage for Residuals

**Mixed waste transformation**:
- Would otherwise be landfilled or incinerated
- Transformed into energy-rich liquids

**The Process**:
- Works on unsorted, dirty, laminated, or hybrid materials
- Converts organic and plastic fractions into fuel, reducing fossil fuel demand
- Allows non-oxidized recovery of metals, even from complex packaging
- Produces no methane, unlike landfills
- Leaves behind mineral-rich char with minimal volume and potential for reuse (e.g., in cement)

**Net Impact**:
Urban RIG acts as a final-stage valorization platform, capturing energy and materials even from what would typically be considered "unrecoverable" in traditional systems. It drastically reduces both landfill dependency and emissions, while simplifying logistics by allowing mixed-stream input.

### A.7.2 Recycling for Plastic

Comparison of traditional plastic recycling methods with Urban RIG approach for processing plastic waste in MSW, focusing on cost, energy, and environmental impact.

**Assumptions**:
- 1 ton (1,000 kg) of plastic in MSW
- Urban RIG uses 70 kg of gas per ton
- Process yields 85% oil and 15% gas from pyrolysis
- Gas is self-generated from the process itself

---

#### Traditional Plastic Recycling

**✅ Advantages**:
- Lower emissions (when done properly): Mechanical recycling doesn't involve combustion or chemical breakdown
- Circular economy: Plastics are reused as raw materials
- Less waste: Diverts plastics from landfills and oceans

**❌ Drawbacks**:
- Selective input: Only clean, sorted, and recyclable plastics (e.g., PET, HDPE) processed — usually <10% of total plastic in MSW
- High contamination rates: Food waste and mixed materials reduce yield
- Energy-intensive: Washing, shredding, melting require significant power
- Costly logistics: Sorting, collection, cleaning are expensive
- Downcycling: Most plastics lose quality and can't be recycled repeatedly

---

#### Urban RIG Approach (Advanced Pyrolysis)

**✅ Advantages**:
- **Handles mixed/dirty plastics**: Can process contaminated, multi-layered, or non-recyclable plastics
- **Self-sustaining energy**:
  - 15% of plastic becomes gas
  - Only 70 kg of gas needed per ton
  - Even from just the gas yield, UR is nearly energy self-sufficient
  - Surplus oil (85%) can be sold or refined into fuels or chemicals
- **Waste-to-value**: Converts waste into usable hydrocarbons
- **Minimizes landfill use**: Captures value from plastics that would otherwise be landfilled or incinerated
- **Carbon benefit**: If oil displaces fossil production, reduces demand for virgin crude

**❌ Drawbacks**:
- Capital cost: High upfront investment in pyrolysis systems
- Air emissions: Some emissions depending on scrubber quality and controls (though better than incineration)
- Carbon output: Oil produced will eventually be burned unless refined into non-fuel products
- Complexity: More complex than mechanical recycling; needs feedstock management and technical oversight

---

#### Summary Comparison Table

| Metric | Traditional Recycling | Urban RIG |
|--------|----------------------|-----------|
| Feedstock compatibility | Clean, sorted plastics only | All plastics incl. dirty, mixed |
| Energy requirement | High (electricity, washing) | Low (70 kg gas/ton, self-generated) |
| Self-sustaining? | No | Yes (gas from plastic powers the system) |
| Oil/gas recovery | None | 85% oil, 15% gas |
| Circular economy | Yes, but limited | Yes, broader (chemical circularity) |
| Environmental impact | Low if clean & effective | Moderate, depends on emissions control |
| Cost efficiency | High processing cost for low yield | Higher capex, lower opex; more value per ton |

---

#### Final Takeaways for Plastics

- **Urban RIG is more robust and efficient** for handling the reality of plastic in MSW — dirty, mixed, often unrecyclable
- It turns waste into fuel, and **the process powers itself**, making it highly energy-efficient
- Compared to traditional recycling (which only captures a small portion), **UR offers higher waste diversion and economic value**
- Main challenges are **environmental regulation compliance** and **initial infrastructure cost**

### A.7.3 Recycling for Food and Organics

If the output of food waste from Urban RIG is average-energy charcoal (biochar), we're converting food waste into a carbon-rich, storable, and useful product.

**Context**:
- 1 ton (1,000 kg) of food waste
- Processed by:
  - Composting (aerobic)
  - Gasification (thermochemical)
  - Urban RIG using 70 kg of gas from pyrolyzed plastic

---

#### Urban RIG with Charcoal Output (Biochar)

**➕ Advantages**

**1. Biochar as Output**
- Energy-dense charcoal (~20–30 MJ/kg)
- Can be used as:
  - Fuel (solid substitute for coal)
  - Soil amendment (retains nutrients, holds water, boosts microbial life)
  - Carbon sequestration agent (very stable C — stays locked in soil for centuries)
- Adds both economic and climate value

**2. Circular Fuel Loop**
- Uses only 70 kg of gas per ton, sourced from plastic pyrolysis
- Gas is free of fossil input, so process is energetically self-reliant once scaled
- Minimal external energy needed

**3. Carbon Capture**
- Up to 50% of carbon in food is converted into solid char
- If char is land-applied → **carbon-negative pathway**
- Composting emits CO₂ and methane; RIG locks it in

**4. Cost & Logistics**
- No compost infrastructure needed
- Modular, scalable units allow on-site processing
- Final product (char) is compact, easy to transport or monetize

**5. Environmental Impact**
- Avoids landfill: no methane, no leachate
- Controlled emissions
- Low odor vs composting
- Potential to upcycle urban organic waste into carbon credits

---

**➖ Drawbacks**

**1. Energy Use**
- Still requires thermal energy — but low and covered by internal system gas
- Moisture in food waste can reduce process efficiency (may need drying/preheating steps)

**2. Char Quality Variability**
- Depends on feedstock and process temp
- May contain impurities (metals, salts from MSW food), limiting agricultural use unless filtered or blended

**3. Market Development Needed**
- Char fuel market exists, but pricing is volatile
- If used as soil amendment, needs regulation, certification, and education to expand use

---

#### Updated Comparison Table

| Factor | Composting | Gasification | Urban RIG w/ Charcoal |
|--------|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| Energy Input | Low | High | Low (70 kg gas/ton from waste plastic) |
| Energy Recovery | No | Yes (syngas) | Yes (solid fuel = charcoal) |

| Carbon Retention | Low (lost as CO₂) | Low | High (C in char is stable for centuries) |
| Emission Control | Needs management | Difficult | Easy (sealed, closed-loop) |
| Footprint | High | Medium | Small, modular |
| Cost | Low-medium | High | Medium (but product adds revenue) |
| Output Use | Compost | Energy | Charcoal (fuel, soil, carbon sink) |
| Circularity | Medium | Low | High (plastic fuels food, char stores carbon) |
| Climate Impact | Mild benefit | Neutral to negative | Strong positive (carbon negative) |

---

#### Summary for Food Waste

With charcoal as a usable, energy-rich output, Urban RIG:
- **Turns food waste into value**, not just waste
- **Acts as a carbon sink**, helping fight climate change
- **Complements plastic pyrolysis** by using its gas output for food processing
- Delivers a **scalable, circular solution** ideal for urban environments with limited space and composting capacity

---

#### Final Verdict

**Urban RIG with biochar production clearly outperforms composting and gasification** on carbon retention, circularity, and urban viability — especially when powered by waste-derived gas.
