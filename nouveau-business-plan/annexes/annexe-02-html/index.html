How the Urban Rig performs: conversion yields, product quality, emissions and energy, from the test reports and the manufacturer's documentation.

Key Messages for Investors

1. The Urban Rig converts plastics into fuel at high rates.

  • In 11 batch runs on clean PE, PP and PS filed with the Chiba prefectural government in 2022, 9 runs produced between 72 % and 82 % of their input weight as liquid oil 2.1.b.
  • The two lower results (61 % and 55 %) were both on polystyrene, which leaves more solid residue 2.1.b.
  • Polyolefins. On PE and PP, the dominant plastics in packaging, oil yields ranged from 73 % to 81 % 2.1.b.
  • Oil plus gas. The process also produces a hydrocarbon gas, which the plant burns as fuel. Counting oil and gas together, 87.8 % to 99.6 % of the PE and PP input was converted into fuel 2.5.a.82.5.a.6.

2. The manufacturer's design figures are consistent with the tests.

  • For a feed of 90 % resin-based waste, OneWorld states a daily output of 76.5 % oil and a further 11.5 % "recovery loss", mainly discharged as flue gas 2.5.f.62.3.c.
  • OneWorld also states an oil recovery of "about 85%" from resin-based waste 2.3.c.
  • OneWorld's 2021 demonstration runs on mixed plastics produced 83 % oil overall 2.5.f.8, with a weight reduction of 96 % 2.3.f.

3. All three products carry energy.

  • Oil. An independent laboratory measured a gross calorific value of 46,000 J/g on oil from municipal packaging plastics, with a density of 0.8642 g/cm³ and a sulphur content below 0.05 % 2.2.a.
  • Gas. From its measured composition, the pyrolysis gas has a net calorific value of 37.4 MJ/kg 2.5.c.12.
  • Char. The char has a calorific value of 32,090 J/g 2.2.l. This is well above the 12,000 kJ/kg minimum for a French solid recovered fuel 2.4.l.

4. The continuous plant runs on its own gas.

  • OneWorld's burner rating corresponds to a heat demand of 1,507 MJ per tonne treated 2.5.d.1.
  • The gas alone provides 2.9 times this demand on a resin-rich feed 2.5.d.4 and 1.2 times on household waste with 10 % resins 2.5.d.3.
  • The oil and the char remain available for sale.

5. Measured emissions are below the current EU limits for waste incineration.

  • Every measured value is below the daily limits of Directive 2010/75/EU, on the values as reported 2.4.a. This covers nitrogen oxides, sulphur oxides, dust, hydrogen chloride and dioxins.
  • Most values are also within or below the stricter best-available-technique levels for new plants 2.4.b (Section 2.7).

6. Points to secure with OneWorld before financial close (Section 2.9.1):

  • performance guarantees for the continuous URC-2000 on the target waste mix;
  • the chlorine level of the oil. The oils made from municipal plastics contain 0.137 % and 0.526 % chlorine 2.2.a, below the 1 % level above which EU rules require waste to be burned at 1,100 °C or more 2.4.i. This suits them for combustion in a permitted plant (Section 2.4.1). According to OneWorld, the chlorine comes from PVC and similar materials in the feed 2.3.h. With a PVC-free feed, or after dechlorination, the oil would open the higher-value outlets as well: chemical recycling, marine fuel and jet fuel. A chlorine measurement on oil from a PVC-free feed is requested to confirm it (R-013).

2.4.3 Oil Composition by GC-MS and Jet Fuel Potential

The Osaka Research Institute of Industrial Science and Technology analysed a liquid sample labelled "drain". The machine, the feed and the date are not stated 2.2.n. The full peak list and its reading are in the full analysis (Annex 2.4.3).

  • What evaporates at 350 °C (46.3 % of the sample 2.5.g.18) is mainly olefins: 57.59 % of the peak area 2.5.g.14. The largest peak is dimethylheptene, with 9 carbon atoms, at 21.26 % 2.2.n.
  • Hydrocarbons with 8 to 16 carbon atoms make 58.54 % of the peak area, of which 38.2 % with a high library match 2.5.g.72.5.g.10. These compounds boil below the 300 °C end point of jet fuel 2.5.g.192.4.q.
  • What stands in the way: possible oxygenated compounds (15.49 % 2.5.g.15), a silicon compound, the chlorine of Section 2.4.1, and 53.7 % of the sample that does not evaporate at 350 °C 2.2.n.

How much could become jet fuel (order of magnitude). Taking the peak area shares as mass shares, a working assumption 2.5.0, about 18 % to 27 % of the sample is made of hydrocarbons in the jet fuel carbon range:

  • low estimate, 17.69 %: only the hydrocarbons identified with a high library match, in the 46.3 % of the sample that evaporates at 350 °C 2.5.g.8;
  • high estimate, 27.1 %: all the named hydrocarbons with 8 to 16 carbon atoms 2.5.g.9.

These shares are before treatment (reading by Claude, not quantified): hydrogenation, the removal of the lightest compounds to meet the flash point and the purification would lower them; cracking the heavy part of the sample could raise them. They apply to one unidentified sample and are an indication for discussion with a refiner, not a yield to put in the financial model.

Reading for sustainable aviation fuel. The 9 to 13 carbon olefins (dimethylheptene, dimethyloctene, undecene, trimethylnonene, tridecene) are the natural core of a jet fuel cut, after hydrogenation, fractionation and purification. No aromatic compound is identified; whether aromatics must be added depends on the approval pathway (not found). Under ReFuelEU Aviation, SAF include recycled carbon aviation fuels, subject to an emissions savings threshold and certification 2.4.r. The analysis is a first indication, not a demonstration: an identified sample, a mass-based analysis and a jet fuel distillation test are needed (R-013).

2.4.4 Char: Calorific Value and Composition

Calorific value. Izumitec Co. measured 32,090 J/g on a urethane char, using the JIS M 8814 calorimeter method 2.2.l. The project treats this value as representative of Urban Rig char 2.5.0. According to OneWorld, the char from food waste, paper and wood is a coal substitute 2.3.a.

Why char matters for municipal waste. On the two municipal runs, char was 11.3 to 13.9 kg per 50 kg of input 2.1.b. In a feed containing paper, wood or food waste, the organic fraction becomes char. 2.7 Exhaust Gas Emissions Compared with EU Standards

2.7.1 Which Standards Apply

The waste incineration chapter of the EU Industrial Emissions Directive excludes pyrolysis plants only if their gases are purified until they are no longer waste and emit no more than natural gas 2.4.a (Article 42). This annex therefore compares the results with the incineration limits, the stricter case. Two levels are used:

  • Current legal limits. The daily average emission limit values of Directive 2010/75/EU, Annex VI, Part 3 2.4.a:

    • dust 10 mg/Nm³ 2.4.a;
    • hydrogen chloride 10 mg/Nm³ 2.4.a;
    • sulphur dioxide 50 mg/Nm³ 2.4.a;
    • nitrogen oxides 200 mg/Nm³, expressed as NO₂, for new plants 2.4.a;
    • carbon monoxide 50 mg/Nm³ 2.4.a;
    • dioxins and furans 0.1 ng/Nm³ 2.4.a.

  • Strictest levels. The best-available-technique emission levels (BAT-AELs) for new waste incineration plants 2.4.b:

    • dust below 2 to 5 mg/Nm³ 2.4.b;
    • hydrogen chloride below 2 to 6 mg/Nm³ 2.4.b;
    • sulphur dioxide 5 to 30 mg/Nm³ 2.4.b;
    • nitrogen oxides 50 to 120 mg/Nm³ 2.4.b;
    • dioxins and furans below 0.01 to 0.04 ng I-TEQ/Nm³ 2.4.b.

Both sets of values apply to dry gas at 11 % oxygen 2.4.a2.4.b. The Japanese laboratories reported concentrations in ppm, at the oxygen content of the sample. They were converted to mg/Nm³ with the Danish EPA factors (1 ppm = 2.05 mg/Nm³ for NO₂, 2.93 for SO₂, 1.63 for HCl) 2.4.d.

2.7.4 Process Water

The process produces water that must be treated before discharge. The Chiba runs produced 931 L of wastewater in total 2.1.d. Untreated samples analysed by Oosumi Co. 2.2.c show:

  • in water from the packaging plastics run: phenols at 740 mg/L and cyanide at 14 mg/L 2.2.c;
  • in water from the non-combustible residue run: a pH of 0.8 and a biochemical oxygen demand of 16,000 mg/L 2.2.c.

The Noda unit was a test unit without a wastewater treatment step. The operator states that the raw water exceeds the discharge standards and that treatment is to be built into the commercial unit 2.1.h.

OneWorld offers the SEA CORAL ceramic purifier as an option 2.3.i. Its documentation reports a seawater desalination test (chloride ion from 31,000 to 31 mg/L) and a capacity of about 330 t of water per day per blower 2.3.j. It contains no test on pyrolysis process water, which carries phenols, cyanide and a very acidic pH (see above), and no laboratory or date 2.3.j. The residual brine (140,000 mg/L of chloride ion in the desalination test 2.3.j) would also need disposal.

According to OneWorld, part of the purified water is reused in the steam boiler 2.3.d. The volume per tonne on a continuous plant and the treatment cost are to be confirmed (R-017) and costed in Annex 9.
