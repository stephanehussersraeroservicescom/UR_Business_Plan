Annex 5. Municipal Solid Waste (France)

This annex describes residual household waste in France (ordures ménagères résiduelles, OMR): how much there is, what it contains, what the Urban Rig can and cannot convert, and what an Urban Rig could produce from one tonne of it. Part 1 covers what goes in; Part 2 covers what comes out. Plastic streams collected on their own are covered by Annex 8, mixed commercial waste by Annex 7 and the CDG airport by Annex 6.

How to Read This Annex

OneWorld, the manufacturer of the Urban Rig, is the sponsor of this business planD-027. Its documentation is marked "OneWorld design data"; these are the company's own figures and have not been verified independently.

Each figure or fact is followed by a small link. It opens the evidence sheet: what the evidence shows, its confidence, the source document and its location, or the calculation. Sheets numbered 5.x belong to this annex; sheets numbered 1.x, 2.x, 3.x and 8.x are those of Annexes 1, 2, 3 and 8. All sheets are listed in the index of evidence sheets.

Composition data. The composition comes from the national characterisation campaign of 2024 (MODECOM 2024), published by ADEME as open data and in a summary report. The full technical report is available only through a registration form and has not been read (request R-042).

No Urban Rig test on household waste. The Urban Rig has been tested on clean plastics and on two municipal plastic-rich streams, not on raw household waste (Annex 2). Part 2 therefore combines those tests, published literature and working assumptions. Each assumption is dated and named; the operator of the model can change it.

Revision. This version was revised after an independent check of the annex. The changes are listed, point by point, in the project's register of discrepancies for Annex 5.

Key Messages

  • Residual household waste is a large, steady stream. France collected 14,752,013 t, 223.46 kg per inhabitant, the 2024 figure of the MODECOM campaign taken from the 2023 collection survey5.L.a. One URC-2000 would take about 1.5 % of the residual household waste of Ile-de-France5.C.a. Ile-de-France collected 3.32 Mt in 2022, 268 kg per inhabitant, and sends 94 % of its residual waste to 18 incinerators5.P.a.
  • It is mostly wet organic matter. Putrescibles make up 33.67 % of the mass5.C.b and sanitary textiles, mainly paper tissues and nappies, 16.99 %5.C.c. The average moisture is 42.1 %, with a wide confidence interval of plus or minus 6.3 points5.P.b5.L.a, about 421 kg of water per tonne5.C.d.
  • Plastics are 15.79 % of the mass5.C.e, about 111 kg of dry plastic per tonne5.C.f. Their split by polymer is not published (request R-045).
  • 11.79 % of the mass cannot be converted: glass, metals and other minerals, which pass through, and hazardous items, which must be removed for safety5.C.g. For one machine, about 6,000 t a year need a pre-sorting step and an outlet5.C.h.
  • Three scenarios, selected on the web pageD-047. Per tonne of residual household waste, plastics and the PP and PE of nappies give 65, 87 or 96 kg of hydrocarbon oil (pessimistic, standard, optimistic)5.C.i5.C.j5.C.kD-044D-048. OneWorld design data state 5 % of oil for household waste with 10 % of resins2.3.a.
  • Nappies and textiles also give gas and a wet liquid: 41.8 to 44.6 kg of gas, a mix of carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, hydrogen and methane, and 18.6 to 23.2 kg of oxygenated liquid per tonne5.C.l5.C.m5.C.n5.C.oD-048.
  • Char is the main solid product. The other organic matter would give 89 to 142 kg of char per tonne, 107 kg in the standard case5.C.p5.C.q5.C.rD-045. Water, 421 kg per tonne, is the largest output5.C.d. Whether the char is sold or paid away, and at what price, is to be decided (Annex 3).
  • About 205 kg per tonne are not yet allocated to a product in the standard case5.C.s: the gas and liquid of food, garden waste, paper and cardboard. They are shown as an indicative split, and the Urban Rig test on household waste must measure them (R-021)D-053.
  • The hydrocarbon gas alone is not enough; full self-sufficiency is undetermined. The hydrocarbon gas gives about 444 MJ per tonne5.C.t, against 1,029 MJ only to evaporate the water5.C.u and 1,507 MJ of burner demand stated by OneWorld, calculated at the nominal 200 t per day2.5.d.1. The gas of the organic matter may reduce the gap, but it has not been measured. Until a test, the model provides a line of char, oil or purchased fuelD-029D-053; by default, the deficit is covered by burning part of the oilD-069, against a burner demand of 2,152 MJ per tonne at 140 t per day1.C.eD-072.
  • Tests are the priority. An Urban Rig test on French residual household waste, with measured moisture, gas and char, is the main request to OneWorld (R-021).

5.5.1 What Happens to a Nappy in the Reactor

No Urban Rig test exists on nappies (request R-021). Published work describes what each component does as the temperature rises. The URC-2000 heats its feed through zones at 150, 300, 450 and 600 °C, according to OneWorld design data2.3.b.

Temperature What happens Evidence
Up to about 230 °C Water leaves. A used nappy is wet: sanitary textiles hold 60.0 % moisture in residual waste5.P.b; the superabsorbent itself loses its bound water, 14 % of its dry mass, below 360 °C5.L.d 5.L.e
About 230 to 550 °C Cellulose (fluff pulp) and paper decompose; the main devolatilisation of household waste components lies between 230 and 550 °C5.L.f. They give char, gas and a wet liquid (Section 5.10) 5.L.f
From about 360 to 400 °C upwards The superabsorbent (sodium polyacrylate) starts to break down: stable up to about 400 °C5.L.g; main losses between 360 and 592 °C5.L.d. It releases water, CO2, CO and methane between 300 and 500 °C5.L.e
About 400 to 500 °C Polypropylene and polyethylene (topsheet, backsheet) crack into oil and gas, as in the Chiba tests (Annex 2) 2.1.b
End A carbon-rich residue remains. For a sodium polyacrylate binder, the residue at 1,000 °C was 11.3 %, mostly carbon, and sodium carbonate formed during the decomposition5.L.e; pure sodium polyacrylate kept 40 % of its mass at 800 °C5.L.d

Results on whole nappies.

  • Microwave pyrolysis of used baby nappies gave up to 43 % oil, 29 % gas and 28 % char by mass (abstract only)5.L.h.
  • Pyrolysis of nappies taken from the rejects of household waste plants maximised hydrogen and methane at 575 °C, up to 23 mmol of non-condensable gas per gram; at 525 °C, over 63 % of the liquid had boiling points close to heavy naphtha; the char had a low surface area, 9.7 m2/g (abstract only)5.L.i.
  • New shredded nappies held 7.5 % ash and 3.9 % fixed carbon; the cellulosic fraction of used, sterilised nappies 10.7 % ash and 1.2 % fixed carbon5.L.j.

Sodium is the operational risk. The superabsorbent brings 2 to 3 % sodium by mass into new nappies5.L.j. With silicon and other minerals, sodium forms low-melting ashes between 500 and 800 °C; the authors observed agglomeration of their reactor bed, a fluidised bed5.L.j. This has not been observed on an Urban Rig, whose reactor is different. In an Urban Rig, this sodium would end in the char and could cause sticky deposits; it also lowers the value of the char as a fuel. This is to be checked in the OneWorld test (R-021).

Reading. A nappy is not mainly an oil feed: its cellulose and superabsorbent give char, water and gas, and its PP and PE a modest amount of oil. Pyrolysis destroys the superabsorbent; recovering it as a material requires separation before any thermal treatment, which is outside the Urban Rig process.

Reading.

  • The hydrocarbon gas does not cover the heat. It covers 0.432 of the heat needed to evaporate the water5.C.by and 0.295 of the burner demand stated by OneWorld5.C.bz. Whether the evaporation is included in the burner demand is not stated by OneWorld. The latent heat leaves out the sensible heat, the superheating of the steam and the losses; the net heat demand cannot be calculated yet.
  • The gas from the organic matter is not counted. The Urban Rig admits no oxygen, so nothing burns in the reactor. Carbon dioxide still forms, because the oxygen is inside the material itself: the cellulosic fraction of used nappies holds 48.0 % oxygen on a dry basis5.L.j and a food waste mixture 49.38 %3.L.ag. Under pure nitrogen, without any oxygen added, this nappy fraction gave a gas of 23.5 to 33.3 % CO2, 27.8 to 33.4 % CO, 7.90 to 10.7 % hydrogen and 14.5 to 14.7 % methane, plus 16.5 to 17.7 % of other hydrocarbons, between 500 and 600 °C (mole fractions of the dry gas)5.L.j. Most of this gas, everything except the CO2, is therefore combustible, but its calorific value per tonne has not been measured on an Urban Rig, and the effect of the superheated steam on this gas is not documented (request R-021).
  • The char could close the gap. The standard char carries 3,429 MJ per tonne at 32.09 MJ/kg5.C.bx, a value measured on a urethane char and assumed for household waste char by the sponsorD-038; it is the high case. At the 24.55 MJ/kg of a food waste char3.C.ag, the prudent comparison, it carries about 2,623 MJ per tonne5.C.ca. Both exceed the burner demand alone, but the useful heat depends on combustion losses and on the ash and moisture of the char, which are not measured. Burning part of the char would reduce the char sold. The model must choose between burning char or oil and buying fuel (Section 5.17).
  • Consistent with Annex 2. Self-sufficiency on household waste is not claimedD-029.

5.12.1 Possible Improvement: Separating the Wet Fraction by Press-Extrusion

Most of the water of residual household waste sits in its food waste and sanitary textiles (Section 5.3). A press-extrusion step before the reactor could remove much of it. This is a possible improvement, not part of the base case; its equipment belongs to Annex 9 (request R-047).

What it would change for the Urban Rig.

  • Better feed. The dry fraction is richer in plastic and holds far less water. The heat needed to evaporate the water, 1,029 MJ per tonne of raw waste5.C.u, would fall accordingly; oil per tonne fed would rise.
  • A second product to manage. The wet paste is not clean water: it holds 30 to 35 % of solids and goes to anaerobic digestion5.L.n. Its outlet and its price are a cost, or a revenue, still to be established. Whether the water released could feed the steam circuit, after treatment by SEA CORAL, is not documented.
  • Data to confirm. The press figures come from a manufacturer and a distributor; the waste they describe held 25 to 30 % of water5.L.n, against 42.1 % for French residual waste5.P.b. A test on French residual waste, measured by a third party, is needed (R-047).

5.17 Recommendations for the Business Plan

  • Present residual household waste as a paid feed with a modest oil yield, not as an oil feed: per tonne received, the standard case gives 87 kg of hydrocarbon oil and 107 kg of char5.C.j5.C.r.
  • Offer the three scenarios as a choice on the web pageD-047; the pessimistic case is the prudent reading until an Urban Rig test on French residual waste existsD-044.
  • Show the energy balance with a purchased fuel or a char consumption line, not as self-sufficientD-029; count the gas of the organic matter only as an optionD-053.
  • Use 32.09 MJ/kg for the char as the high case and 24.55 MJ/kg as the prudent case until measuredD-0383.C.ag.
  • Treat a pre-sorting step that recovers metals and removes hazardous items as part of the household waste configuration (Annex 9).
  • Study press-extrusion before the reactor as an option, once a test on French waste and a price are available (Section 5.12.1, Annex 9).
